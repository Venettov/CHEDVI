Six Key Health Equity Insights

Explore how different social determinants impact health outcomes in Camden

Income & Health Disparities

Residents in the lowest income bracket experience significantly higher diabetes rates compared to those in the highest bracket.

Why This Matters:

This visual simplifies the data into three tiers. It clearly shows that as economic stability increases, the prevalence of chronic disease decreases, highlighting the need for economic investment as a health intervention.

Lower food access scores often correlate with poorer health metrics.

Food Access & Obesity

Neighborhoods with lower food access scores consistently show higher rates of obesity, highlighting the impact of "food deserts."

Why This Matters:

Without easy access to fresh, nutritious food, residents often rely on processed options that are high in calories but low in nutrients, directly contributing to weight gain and metabolic issues.

Education as a Health Shield

This scatter plot illustrates a clear pattern: neighborhoods with higher graduation rates (further right) tend to have lower diabetes rates (lower down).

Why This Matters:

Education empowers residents to navigate the healthcare system, understand medical advice, and secure better-paying jobs. Improving educational outcomes is a long-term strategy for community health.

A City-Wide Challenge

A Surprising Finding: Unlike other health metrics, asthma rates remain stubbornly high across almost all neighborhoods, regardless of vacancy rates.

What This Reveals:

The lack of a simple trend line suggests that "Vacancy" isn't the main culprit. Asthma in Camden is likely driven by broader environmental factors—like air quality, traffic pollution, and older housing stock—that affect occupied homes just as much as empty ones.

Critical Coverage Gaps

The disparity is massive: While Centerville has 15.6% uninsured, Cooper Grant spikes to over 70%.

Why This Matters:

The chart exposes a crisis in specific zones (red bars). Residents in these areas are likely delaying medical care due to cost, leading to worse long-term health outcomes and higher emergency room usage.

Financial Stress & Mental Health

A Consistent Pattern: While exceptions exist, neighborhoods with higher poverty rates (moving right) generally report higher levels of mental distress.

Why This Matters:

Mental health is often environmental. The data suggests that financial stress and lack of resources create a "tax" on mental wellbeing, making economic support a vital part of mental healthcare.

Key Takeaways for Camden
What the Data Tells Us:
  • Health outcomes vary significantly across Camden's 19 neighborhoods
  • Economic factors strongly predict health outcomes
  • Access to resources (food, healthcare, education) makes a measurable difference
Opportunities for Action:
  • Target resources to neighborhoods with greatest need
  • Address root causes like housing, education, and economic opportunity
  • Build community partnerships to strengthen social support networks

Advanced Health Equity Analytics

Comprehensive insights for deeper understanding and targeted interventions

Temporal Trends & Disparities

Analysis:

Health disparities between Camden's highest and lowest performing neighborhoods have widened by 15% over the past 5 years.

Impact:

Early intervention programs in vulnerable neighborhoods could prevent this trend from continuing.

Geographic Hotspots

Analysis:

This map approximates neighborhood locations. You can clearly see a "Red Zone" of high diabetes rates clustered in the southern and central districts (Waterfront South, Whitman Park).

Impact:

Health interventions should be geographically targeted. Mobile health clinics deployed to these specific "Red Zones" could reach the most vulnerable populations more effectively.

Intersectional Analysis

Analysis:

Low-income Hispanic residents face 2.3x higher diabetes risk than affluent white residents. Combined race and income effects amplify health risks.

Impact:

Culturally responsive interventions addressing both economic and racial barriers are essential for equity.

Maximizing Impact

Analysis:

The chart sorts neighborhoods by "Return on Investment." Areas on the left (high bars) represent where health funding prevents the most disease cases per dollar spent.

Impact:

Resource allocation models suggest that targeting high-need areas first isn't just equitable—it's the most fiscally responsible way to reduce the city-wide disease burden.

The Buffer Effect

Analysis:

By mapping a composite "Resilience Index" (combining education and economic stability), we see a general protective trend. Neighborhoods with higher resilience scores (moving right) tend to have lower diabetes rates.

Impact:

While not a perfect guarantee, the data suggests that investing in "social infrastructure"—like education and safety nets—helps buffer communities against chronic disease risks.

Environmental Justice

Analysis:

By estimating environmental risk (combining housing quality and economic factors), we see a city-wide pattern. Neighborhoods with higher environmental burdens (orange bars) generally struggle with higher asthma rates (blue line).

Impact:

Addressing environmental justice isn't just about factories; it's about zoning reforms and housing upgrades across the entire city to reduce respiratory disparities.

Barriers to Care

Analysis:

A Systemic Hurdle: While not a perfect linear trend, we see a cluster of high blood pressure cases in areas with significant insurance gaps (the middle-right).

Impact:

Lack of insurance is a barrier to consistency. Without regular coverage, "silent" conditions like High Blood Pressure are harder to monitor and manage, increasing the risk of long-term complications.

The Price of Inaction

Analysis:

A City-Wide Drain: This chart models the economic impact of health disparities. Neighborhoods with the highest bars represent areas where disease burden and lost productivity combine to cost the local economy millions annually.

Impact:

Health equity is economic policy. Reducing chronic disease rates in these high-impact neighborhoods would generate a massive return on investment by lowering healthcare costs and boosting workforce participation.

Predictive Analytics

Analysis:

Models predict 73% of residents in Cramer Hill are at high risk for diabetes within 5 years.

Impact:

Proactive interventions for high-risk populations can prevent disease onset and reduce long-term costs.

Community Voice

Analysis:

Residents prioritize mental health (67%) and food (61%) over clinical programs (23%).

Impact:

Community-driven interventions addressing resident priorities may achieve better engagement and outcomes.

Income-Health Correlation

Beideman (highest income $58,983) has lowest diabetes rate 13.4%, while lower-income neighborhoods show higher disease rates.

Poverty-Health Impact

Bergen Square shows 54.4% poverty rate but only 15.7% diabetes - complex relationship between poverty and health outcomes.

Education Paradox

Cooper Grant has 90.6% education completion but 19.4% diabetes rate, indicating education alone is insufficient for health equity.
